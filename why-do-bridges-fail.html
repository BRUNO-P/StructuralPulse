Why Do Bridges Fail? A Look at the Data

Author: Bruno Pompei | Published: Aug 2026 | 3 min read

Bridges · Structural Assessment · Construction

Bridges are engineered to perform reliably across multi-decade service lives, yet structural collapses continue to occur globally. Synthesizing data on historical failures provides critical insight into recurring structural vulnerabilities and high-risk operational phases.

Top Causes of Bridge Failures

Global collapse data indicates that three primary drivers account for nearly 70% of all recorded bridge failures combined:

  • Natural Disasters (23.9%): Seismic events, extreme windstorms, and slope instability stress even well-analyzed structures beyond ultimate limit states.
  • Construction Mistakes (22.6%): Execution errors, temporary works instability, and falsework failures during building phases create persistent long-term structural flaws.
  • Hydraulic Issues (21.1%): Pier/abutment scour and severe flood events undermine foundations, serving as a primary failure mode worldwide.
  • Collisions (10.1%): Impact loads from maritime vessels, heavy vehicle strike actions, and derailments induce unpredicted local collapse.
  • Overload & Material Degradation (9.7% / 5.5%): Increasing axle loads and environmental degradation (corrosion, fatigue) systematically reduce residual capacity over time.
  • Design Errors (4.5%) & Lack of Maintenance (2.6%): Calculation/modelling oversights and neglected intervention cycles complete the failure distribution.

Most Affected Bridge Types

Failure rates vary significantly across structural forms due to population prevalence and load-path redundancy:

  • Beam Bridges (45.0%): Representing the vast majority of built stock globally, simple and continuous beam configurations record the highest absolute frequency of failures.
  • Arch Bridges (20.0%): Highly durable under compression, but particularly vulnerable to foundation displacement caused by hydraulic scour.
  • Cable-Stayed & Suspension Bridges (5.0% each): While total failure rates remain low, collapses in long-span cable-supported structures are high-consequence events driven by dynamic wind action or cable fatigue.

Key Engineering Implications

Designing robust bridge infrastructure requires looking beyond statutory load combinations. Safe long-term performance relies on prioritizing three core areas:

  • Foundation Protection: Implementing comprehensive scour monitoring and hydrodynamic protection around sub-structures.
  • Construction Stage Checks: Conducting rigorous non-linear verification of temporary loading phases and execution quality control.
  • Asset Lifecycle Management: Tracking cumulative material fatigue, environmental corrosion, and actual operational load growth.
